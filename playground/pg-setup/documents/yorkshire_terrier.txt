Yorkshire Terrier

The Yorkshire Terrier, often called the Yorkie, is a tiny terrier that was developed in Yorkshire, England, in the 19th century. Working-class textile mill workers bred the Yorkie to catch rats in mills and mines. The breed later became a fashionable lap dog among Victorian ladies, and today it is one of the most popular toy breeds.

A Yorkshire Terrier weighs 4 to 7 pounds and stands 7 to 8 inches tall. Its most notable feature is its long, silky, straight coat, which is a steel blue on the back and tan on the face and chest. The coat has hair-like texture similar to human hair and has no undercoat, so Yorkies shed very little.

Many Yorkie owners keep the coat long and brush it daily, while others choose a shorter cut for easier upkeep. Yorkies need regular grooming and baths, and their fine hair tangles easily. The puppy coat is usually black and tan and changes color as the dog matures.

The temperament of the Yorkshire Terrier is feisty, brave, and confident. Despite its size, the Yorkie keeps the determined terrier spirit of its rat-catching ancestors and often acts as though it were much larger than it is. Yorkies can be barky and may chase small animals, so early training and socialization are important.

A Yorkshire Terrier named Smoky became one of the most famous war dogs in history. Found in a foxhole in the Pacific during World War II, Smoky went on to serve with a U.S. Army soldier, helping to run a telegraph wire through a narrow pipe under an airfield, and later worked as a therapy dog visiting hospitals.

Yorkshire Terriers need moderate exercise, such as short daily walks and playtime. They usually live 11 to 15 years.

Common health concerns for the Yorkshire Terrier include patellar luxation, a collapsing trachea, dental disease, and a liver condition called portosystemic shunt. Using a harness rather than a collar can help protect the Yorkie's delicate windpipe.
